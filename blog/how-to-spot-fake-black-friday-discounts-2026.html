Quick answer

A Black Friday discount is only real if the price you pay is lower than what the item normally sold for in the weeks before the sale, from the same seller, for the same model, once shipping and fees are added. To check, look up the item's price history, compare the exact model number at two or three other stores, and read the return policy before you pay. If the "was" price was never actually charged, the discount is decoration, not a saving.

  • Compare the sale price with the recent selling price, not the "list" or "compare at" price
  • Add shipping, service fees and any required add-ons to get the real total
  • Read the return window, who pays return shipping, and any final-sale exclusions
  • Pay with a method that gives you dispute rights, and buy from sites you can verify

Key takeaways

  • The size of a percentage-off badge tells you little. What matters is the gap between the sale price and the price the item actually sold for recently.
  • Price history is the single most useful check. It takes a minute or two and settles most "is this a real deal?" questions.
  • Return rules differ by store and by country. A slightly higher price with a generous return window can be the better buy, especially for gifts.
  • Fake shops and payment scams rise around big shopping events. How you pay matters as much as what you pay.

Black Friday and Cyber Monday 2026 dates

In 2026, US Thanksgiving falls on Thursday, November 26, which puts Black Friday on Friday, November 27 and Cyber Monday on Monday, November 30. Many European retailers also run Black Friday promotions on the same Friday, even though Thanksgiving itself is not observed there. In practice, a lot of stores now start "Black Week" or early-access sales well before the Friday and stretch them past Cyber Monday, so the exact start and end dates depend on the retailer.

Key shopping dates for late November and early December 2026
Date Event What to keep in mind
Thursday, November 26 Thanksgiving (US) Some online sales go live early; check whether "early access" needs a paid membership or sign-up.
Friday, November 27 Black Friday The busiest day for deal headlines. Check price history before you trust a big percentage.

The dates matter mainly for returns and delivery: a gift bought on November 27 may need to come back before you have even wrapped it, so read them alongside the return section below.

What a fake discount looks like

Most misleading deals are not outright lies. They rely on how prices are framed, so that an ordinary price looks like a bargain. These are the patterns worth recognizing.

An inflated "was" or "compare at" price

The most common trick is a crossed-out reference price that the item rarely or never sold for. Labels such as "list price", "compare at", "RRP" or "original price" can refer to a manufacturer's suggested price that almost nobody charges. A discount measured against that number looks large but may be close to the normal everyday price. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission's Guides Against Deceptive Pricing say a former-price comparison should be based on a price at which the item was openly and actively offered, in good faith, for a reasonably substantial period. That guides sellers; it does not replace your own check.

Prices raised just before the sale

Another pattern is a price that quietly rises in the weeks before a sale event and then drops back to roughly where it started, now labeled as a discount. You can only spot this with price history. In the European Union, rules introduced by the Omnibus Directive require that, when a seller announces a price reduction, the "prior price" shown must be the lowest price it applied during at least the 30 days before the reduction. If you shop from EU stores, that reference price is a useful starting point, but you should still compare with other sellers.

"Up to" headlines

"Up to 70% off" means at least one item somewhere is discounted by that much. It says nothing about the item you want. Treat the headline as an invitation to look, then judge each product on its own price.

Special models and bundles that resist comparison

Around big sales, some products appear in versions that are hard to compare: a different model number, a store-exclusive color, an older generation, or a bundle that includes accessories you did not plan to buy. Always compare the exact model or product code, and if a bundle is involved, ask whether you would have bought each piece on its own.

Countdown timers and scarcity banners

Timers, "only 3 left" counters and "12 people are viewing this" messages are designed to stop you from comparing. Some reflect real stock or real deadlines; some reset every time the page loads. Either way, they do not change whether the price is good.

Fees that appear at checkout

A low item price can be offset by shipping, "handling" or service fees, paid gift wrap, or a minimum order value. Some offers also require a membership or sign-up. Compare the total you will actually pay, not the number on the product tile. Our guide to comparing products and stores walks through how to line up totals across different storefronts.

How to check price history before you buy

Price history is the most reliable way to tell a real deal from a relabeled normal price. You do not need special skills; you need a few minutes and a consistent method.

  1. Write down the exact item. Note the brand, the full model or product code, size, color and any capacity or storage figure. This is what you will search for everywhere else.
  2. Look up a price history chart. For some large marketplaces, free price-tracking tools such as camelcamelcamel and Keepa show how an item's price has moved over time. Some shopping search tools also show price trends for certain products. Coverage varies, so treat any single chart as one data point.
  3. Start your own record now. If no chart exists, save the product page, add it to a wishlist, or take a dated screenshot of the price today. Even a week or two of your own notes beats relying on the store's "was" price.
  4. Compare two or three other stores. Search the exact model code, not the product name. If several stores show a similar price, the "deal" may simply be the market price.
  5. Check the unit price. For consumables, multipacks and food gifts, compare the price per item, per 100 grams or per liter. A bigger pack is not always cheaper per unit.
  6. Add the full cost. Include shipping, fees, and the cost of returning the item if it does not work out. For cross-border orders, check whether duties or taxes may apply.
  7. Decide on your own walk-away price. Once you know the normal range, set the price at which you will buy. If the sale does not reach it, you have lost nothing by waiting.

Return windows, holiday policies and the fine print

A good price can turn into a bad purchase if you cannot return the item. Return rules are especially important for gifts, because the person using the item is not the person who chose it, and they may not open it until weeks after delivery.

When does the return window start?

Some stores count the return window from the order date, others from the delivery date. Some large retailers offer extended holiday return periods for items bought in November and December, but the length, the cut-off dates and the excluded categories vary from store to store and year to year. Read the current policy on the store's own website rather than assuming last year's terms still apply.

United States and European Union rules differ

In the United States, there is no general federal law that gives you the right to return an item simply because you changed your mind. Returns depend on the store's own policy, which is why reading it before you buy matters. Separately, the FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule requires sellers to ship within the time they state, or within 30 days if they state no time, and to tell you about delays and offer you the option to cancel.

In the European Union, consumers buying online from a business generally have a 14-day right of withdrawal, counted from the day they receive the goods, without needing to give a reason. There are exceptions, such as personalized items, perishable goods and sealed hygiene products once unsealed. The store must explain how to withdraw, and its terms set out who pays return shipping.

Fine print worth reading before you pay

  • Final sale or non-returnable labels. Some deep discounts come with no returns at all. Decide whether the saving is worth that risk.
  • Return shipping and restocking fees. A free return and a return that costs you shipping plus a fee are very different.
  • Marketplace sellers. On large marketplaces, an item may be sold by a third-party seller with its own return terms. Check who the seller is, not just which website you are on.
  • Gift receipts. If a store offers gift receipts, ask for one. It lets the recipient exchange or return without seeing the price.

Paying safely and avoiding fake shops

Big sales attract scammers because shoppers expect low prices and act quickly.

Verify the shop before you trust the price

  • Type the address yourself or use a bookmark, rather than clicking a link in an ad, text message or email that promises an unusually good deal.
  • Check the domain carefully. Look-alike addresses with extra words, hyphens or unusual endings are a common trick.
  • Look for real contact details, a company name, a physical address and clear policies. Missing or vague information is a warning sign.
  • Search the shop name with words like "reviews" or "complaints" and read independent sources, not just testimonials on the shop's own site.

Choose a payment method with protection

In the United States, the Fair Credit Billing Act lets you dispute certain credit card charges, including charges for goods that were not delivered as agreed. You generally need to write to the card issuer within 60 days after the statement showing the charge was sent. Debit cards and other payment methods often have weaker protections, and dispute rules outside the US vary, so check with your provider.

Avoid paying by bank transfer, wire, gift card or cryptocurrency for online purchases from shops you do not know. The FTC warns that scammers often ask for these methods because the money is hard to recover.

Holiday gift buying checklist

Use this from now until your last order of the season.

  1. List the people and set a total budget. Decide on an amount for each person before you see any sale banners.
  2. Note sizes, preferences and what they already own. For phones, chargers and accessories, confirm the device model and connector type.
  3. Record normal prices now. For each idea, write down the current price at two stores so you can judge sale prices later.
  4. Check delivery cut-offs. Find each store's last recommended order date for holiday delivery, and allow extra time for cross-border shipping.
  5. Confirm the return window covers the holidays. Note whether it starts at order or delivery, and whether a gift receipt is available.
  6. Compare the total, not the tile. Include shipping, fees, gift wrap and any duties for international orders.
  7. Save every confirmation. Keep order emails, receipts and screenshots of the deal page in one folder until the return window has closed.
  8. Track and inspect deliveries. Open and check items soon after they arrive, even if you will wrap them later, so problems surface while you can still return them.
  9. Stop when the list is done. Unplanned "deals" bought after your list is complete are where most overspending happens.

Limitations and checks

This guide explains general methods for judging discounts, returns and payment safety. It cannot tell you whether a specific deal is genuine on a specific day, because prices, stock and policies change, sometimes several times during a sale event. Nothing here is a price prediction.

The legal points summarized above are simplified overviews, not legal advice. Rules on returns, refunds, pricing and card disputes depend on where you live, where the seller is based and how you paid. For details, check the store's policy, your card issuer or your local consumer protection authority.

Bottom line

Spotting a fake Black Friday discount comes down to three questions: what did this exact item sell for recently, what will I pay in total, and what happens if I need to send it back? Price history answers the first, a trip to the final checkout step answers the second, and the return policy answers the third. Make your list before November 27, record normal prices now, and let deals that fail your checks go. A missed "bargain" costs nothing; a non-returnable impulse buy does.

Frequently asked questions

When is Black Friday 2026?

Black Friday 2026 is on Friday, November 27, the day after US Thanksgiving on Thursday, November 26. Cyber Monday follows on Monday, November 30. Many retailers start sales earlier and run them for a week or more, so check each store's own dates.

How can I tell if a Black Friday discount is real?

Compare the sale price with what the exact same model actually sold for in the weeks before the sale, using a price history chart, your own saved prices, or current prices at two or three other stores. Then add shipping and fees. If the total is not meaningfully lower than the normal selling price, the discount is mostly in the labeling.

Are Cyber Monday deals better than Black Friday deals?

Not as a rule. Many offers overlap or simply continue from Black Friday into Cyber Monday, and some stores change prices several times across the weekend. Judge each item against its own price history rather than assuming one day is better.

Can I return a Black Friday purchase if I change my mind?

It depends on the store and where you live. In the United States, returns for a change of mind depend on the retailer's own policy. In the European Union, online purchases from a business generally come with a 14-day right of withdrawal from delivery, with some exceptions such as personalized or perishable items. Always read the return policy before you pay, especially for items marked final sale.

What is the safest way to pay during holiday sales?

A credit card or a payment service with a clear dispute process is usually the safer choice for online purchases, because it gives you a way to challenge charges for goods that do not arrive or are not as described. Avoid paying unfamiliar shops by bank transfer, wire, gift card or cryptocurrency, since those payments are hard to recover.
